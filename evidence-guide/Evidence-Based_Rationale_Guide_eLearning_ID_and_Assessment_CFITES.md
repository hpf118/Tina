# Evidence-Based Rationale Guide: E-Learning Instructional Design & Assessment Good Practices (CFITES-Aligned)

Nov 1, 2026 · Mike MA

## How to use this guide

This guide gives the theoretical and research basis for every criterion on the *E-Learning Instructional Design and Assessment Good-Practice Review* form. Use it to explain **why** a practice you observed is good, and to cite the source behind that judgement in APA 7th style.

The guide follows the form: six domains, 29 criteria, then the Overall Good-Practice Summary. Criteria are numbered (for example, 4.3 = Domain 4, third criterion) so review notes can point to them.

### Structure of each entry

1.  **Criterion** — the wording on the form, plus a suggested revision where the wording can be sharpened.

2.  **CFITES link** — the CFITES phase and product where the criterion is decided or checked.

3.  **What good practice looks like** — observable indicators a reviewer can find in a storyboard, module or assessment.

4.  **Theoretical foundation** — the theories and studies behind the criterion, with verbatim quotations and page numbers.

5.  **Interpretation: why this is good practice** — how the theory translates into the design decision.

6.  **Strength of evidence** — how strong the backing is (scale below).

7.  **Reviewer prompts and model note** — questions to ask, and a sample note for the “Reusable example or note” cell.

### Strength-of-evidence scale

| Rating                    | Meaning                                                                                    | Typical sources                                                              |
|---------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Strong                    | Replicated experiments and meta-analyses point the same way                                | Meta-analyses, systematic reviews, large experimental programmes             |
| Moderate                  | Sound theory plus consistent but less extensive empirical support                          | Established theories with multiple studies; consensus professional standards |
| Practice- or policy-based | Required by policy, law or professional standards, or supported mainly by expert consensus | CFITES, Official Languages Act, Accessible Canada Act, WCAG, ISO, IBSTPI     |

A practice- or policy-based rating does not mean the criterion is weak. It means the justification is a requirement or a professional standard rather than a learning experiment, and the note should cite it that way.

### Quotation and citation conventions

- Text in quotation marks is quoted verbatim, with a page, section or paragraph number, and was checked against the original or a reputable reproduction.

- Where a page number could not be confirmed, the idea is **paraphrased** and labelled as a paraphrase. Do not convert a paraphrase into a quotation.

- Full references are in the References section, in APA 7th format.

- Statutes and Government of Canada instruments are cited to the official online version; check the current version before formal use, because these instruments are amended.

## Theoretical framework

The checklist rests on three foundations. CFITES doctrine defines the process and the principles. The learning sciences explain why specific design choices work. The modern validity framework tells us whether an assessment supports the decision made from it.

### Foundation 1: CFITES (now the CAF IT&E System)

**A note on the name.** In December 2025, DAOD 5031-2 was retitled *Canadian Armed Forces Individual Training and Education System* (National Defence, 2025b). The A-P9-050 manual series, titled CFITES, is still the cited doctrine. This guide uses “CFITES” because that term appears on the form and in the manuals. In formal documents after December 2025, consider writing “CAF IT&E System (CFITES)”.

**Aim.** DAOD 5031-2 states: “The aim of the CAF IT&E System is to produce the required quantity of qualified military personnel for operational and institutional employment” (National Defence, 2025b, para. 4.1). Every criterion in the checklist serves that aim: qualified people, produced efficiently.

**Five principles.** DAOD 5031-2 requires that “All IT&E activities are to be managed in accordance with CAF IT&E System doctrine, which is grounded in the following five principles” (National Defence, 2025b, para. 4.11): Performance Orientation, Systems Thinking, Systematic Process, Optimum Efficiency and Continuous Improvement. The table below shows how the checklist domains express those principles. The mapping is this guide’s interpretation, not text from the DAOD.

Two official definitions anchor the analysis and design products:

A published CAF training plan shows the chain from QS to lesson: “This TP describes the Enabling Objectives and teaching points that are derived from the Performance Objectives set out in the QS” (Canadian Defence Academy, 2013, Foreword, para. 2).

### Foundation 3: The assessment validity frame

An assessment is good only if it supports the decision made from it. In CFITES that decision is usually: *has this learner achieved this PO or EO to the standard?* Performance Checks are mastery tests; one CAF training plan describes them as “single-level tests that confirm mastery of the PO” (Canadian Defence Academy, 2013, Ch. 3, para. 24).

**Validity is about interpretations and uses, not tests.** “Validity refers to the degree to which evidence and theory support the interpretations of test scores for proposed uses of tests” (American Educational Research Association \[AERA\] et al., 2014, p. 11). “Validity is a unitary concept” (AERA et al., 2014, p. 14). Messick’s classic definition is “an integrated evaluative judgment of the degree to which empirical evidence and theoretical rationales support the adequacy and appropriateness of inferences and actions based on test scores or other modes of assessment” (Messick, 1989, p. 13, as cited in Shepard, 1993, p. 406).

**Two threats to validity (Messick).** Every assessment criterion in Domain 4 guards against one or both:

- **Construct underrepresentation:** “the degree to which a test fails to capture important aspects of the construct” (AERA et al., 2014, p. 12). In Messick’s words, “the assessment is too narrow and fails to include important dimensions or facets of the construct” (Messick, 1994, p. 4). *E-learning example:* a PO requires a learner to carry out a procedure, but the test only asks them to recall its steps.

- **Construct-irrelevant variance:** “the degree to which test scores are affected by processes that are extraneous to the test’s intended purpose” (AERA et al., 2014, p. 12). *E-learning example:* a confusing drag-and-drop interface or heavy reading load lowers scores for reasons unrelated to competence.

**Argument-based validation and “plausibility” (Kane).** “To validate an interpretation or use of test scores is to evaluate the plausibility of the claims based on the scores” (Kane, 2013, p. 1). Validation is “an evaluation of the coherence and completeness of this interpretation/use argument and of the plausibility of its inferences and assumptions”, and “more-ambitious claims require more support than less-ambitious claims” (Kane, 2013, p. 1). Kane describes the argument as a chain of inferences (Kane, 2013; paraphrased). The table applies that chain to a CFITES Performance Check.

| Inference in the argument | Claim that must be plausible                                                                             | Checklist criteria that supply the evidence                               |
|---------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Scoring                   | Each response is scored correctly against a clear key, checklist or rubric                               | 4.3 Fairness and clarity, 5.6 Development clarity                         |
| Generalization            | The items are a large enough, representative sample that another set of items would give the same result | 4.2 Assessment alignment, 4.5 Remediation (alternate forms)               |
| Extrapolation             | Performance on the check reflects performance in the real job context                                    | 3.6 Authenticity and transfer, 4.2, 6.3 Evaluation and transfer readiness |
| Decision                  | The pass/fail standard and its consequences are justified                                                | 4.1 Assessment purpose, proposed criterion on scoring and cut scores      |

**Five sources of validity evidence.** The *Standards* list evidence based on test content, response processes, internal structure, relations to other variables, and consequences of testing (AERA et al., 2014, pp. 14–19). For e-learning review, content evidence (the blueprint matches the objectives) and response-process evidence (learners answer by using the targeted skill, not test-taking tricks) are the two a design reviewer can most directly check.

**Two meanings of “plausibility”.** In validity theory, plausibility refers to the claims made from scores (Kane, 2013). In item writing it refers to distractors: item-writing guidelines include making all distractors plausible, ideally based on learners’ typical errors (Haladyna et al., 2002; paraphrased). Both serve the same goal: a score that means what we say it means.

## Domain 1: Performance Relevance and Learner Context

Domain 1 checks that the learning exists for a real job requirement, fits the people who will take it, and contains only what they need. It is the CFITES *Performance Orientation* principle in practice (National Defence, 2025b, para. 4.11). If Domain 1 fails, good design elsewhere cannot rescue the product: a well-built course on the wrong content still fails its purpose.

### 1.1 Validated purpose and relevance

**Criterion.** The learning addresses an identified performance or organizational requirement and focuses on what learners need to perform.

**Suggested revision.** Name the evidence: “…addresses a requirement confirmed through needs assessment and analysis (for example, the QS and its POs) and focuses on what learners need to perform on the job.”

**CFITES link.** Needs Assessment (Vol. 2) and Analysis (Vol. 3). The QS is “derived from Military Employment Structure requirements” (National Defence, 2025a, para. 2). DAOD 5031-2 defines individual training and education as “A set of activities intended to develop the competencies, skills, knowledge and attitudes a CAF member requires to perform assigned tasks, correctly interpret information and exercise sound judgement” (National Defence, 2025b, para. 2).

**What good practice looks like**

- The design document cites the source requirement: the QS, PO numbers, or a needs-assessment finding.

- Each module states which job task it prepares the learner to do.

- Content is framed around tasks and decisions (“When you receive a defect report, you will…”) rather than topics (“History of the reporting system”).

**Theoretical foundation**

- **Training is one solution among several.** Performance-analysis theory holds that a gap between required and actual performance should be diagnosed before training is chosen. Mager and Pipe’s method asks whether the gap is a skill deficiency at all, or is caused by consequences, obstacles or missing resources that training cannot fix (Mager & Pipe, 1997; paraphrased).

- **Worthy performance.** Gilbert defined performance in terms of valuable accomplishments rather than activity, so training is justified by the job results it supports (Gilbert, 1978; paraphrased).

- **Training needs analysis.** A major review of training science stresses that training is not always the best answer to a performance problem and that a well-conducted training needs analysis can show when a non-training solution is better (Salas et al., 2012; paraphrased).

- **Start from results.** The Kirkpatrick model’s authors emphasize “the importance of planning from Level 4 to Level 1 (or starting with outcomes and working backward to design for them)” (Kirkpatrick Partners, n.d.).

- **The CFITES test of purpose.** “Validation seeks to determine whether the objectives of IT&E satisfy the job-performance requirement for which the program was initially produced” (National Defence, 2009c, para. 4). A course without a traceable requirement cannot pass that test.

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** A validated purpose is the first link in the chain from job requirement to objective to assessment to qualification. It protects against two common failures: training for a problem training cannot solve, and training that covers a topic without improving performance. Framing content around tasks also makes later validation possible.

**Strength of evidence.** Moderate (performance-analysis and training-science literature) and policy-based (CFITES Performance Orientation; QS-based design).

**Reviewer prompts**

- Which QS, PO or needs-assessment finding does this module serve?

- Is the gap really a knowledge or skill gap?

- Could a learner explain which job task each module prepares them for?

**Model note.** *The design document traces Module 2 to PO 403 in the QS and to a needs-assessment finding of repeated reporting errors; each lesson opens with the job task it supports. Reuse as a model of a validated, performance-oriented purpose (Mager & Pipe, 1997; National Defence, 2025a).*

### 1.2 Learner and context fit

**Criterion.** The design reflects entry knowledge, experience, accessibility needs, official-language needs, and the delivery environment.

**Suggested revision.** Add how differences are handled: “…and provides for differences among learners (for example, refreshers for novices, test-out or skip options for experienced members).”

**CFITES link.** Analysis (description of the target population) and Design. The OL requirement is explicit: training must be “planned and provided to CAF members in the OL of their choice” (National Defence, 2025b, para. 5.7). Recognition of prior learning is governed by DAOD 5031-1 (National Defence, 2025a).

**What good practice looks like**

- The design document profiles the target learners: rank or occupation, prerequisites, typical experience, language profile, known accessibility needs.

- It describes the delivery context: device, network, location, time available, whether learners work alone or with a supervisor.

- Prerequisite knowledge is checked or briefly reviewed at the start.

- Experienced learners can demonstrate mastery and skip, where policy allows.

**Theoretical foundation**

- **Prior knowledge.** Ausubel’s often-quoted principle: “The most important single factor influencing learning is what the learner already knows. Ascertain this and teach him accordingly” (Ausubel, 1968, p. vi).

- **Expertise reversal effect.** “Instructional techniques that are highly effective with inexperienced learners can lose their effectiveness and even have negative consequences when used with more experienced learners” (Kalyuga et al., 2003, p. 23). Therefore “to be efficient, instructional design should be tailored to the level of experience of intended learners” (Kalyuga et al., 2003, p. 29).

- **Load depends on the learner.** “Complexity or element interactivity depends on a combination of both the nature of the information and the knowledge of the person processing the information” (Sweller et al., 2019, p. 264).

- **Context.** Tessmer and Richey argued that context is a multilevel set of interacting physical, social and instructional factors, and distinguished the orienting context (the learner’s goals and expectations), the instructional context (where learning happens) and the transfer context (where it will be used) (Tessmer & Richey, 1997; paraphrased).

- **Learner variability.** “The UDL framework emphasizes the idea of learner variability” (CAST, 2024, Engagement section).

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** The same design can help one learner and hinder another. Detailed guidance helps novices but can slow and frustrate experts, so the design must be built for the actual audience. Accessibility and official-language needs are part of that audience profile, not add-ons. The delivery environment determines what is feasible: a long video is useless on a ship with low bandwidth.

**Strength of evidence.** Strong for prior knowledge and expertise reversal; policy-based for official languages and accessibility.

**Reviewer prompts**

- Who exactly is the learner, and what do they already know?

- Is the design suitable for both the least and the most experienced members of the audience?

- Will it work on the devices, networks and schedules learners actually have?

**Model note.** *The design document profiles two learner groups (new entrants and re-qualifying members) and offers a pre-test that lets re-qualifying members skip EOs 1–2. Reuse as a model of learner-tailored design (Kalyuga et al., 2003).*

### 1.3 Content necessity

**Criterion.** Content is relevant to the intended outcomes and avoids unnecessary or unsupported information.

**CFITES link.** Design (selection of teaching points in the TP) and the *Optimum Efficiency* principle (National Defence, 2025b, para. 4.11).

**Scope note.** Criterion 1.3 concerns **what** content is included (curriculum scope). Criteria 5.1 and 5.2 concern **how** it is presented. A module can fail 1.3 with excellent media, or pass 1.3 with poor media.

**What good practice looks like**

- Every teaching point traces to an EO; nothing exists only because it is “interesting”.

- “Nice-to-know” background is cut or moved to optional resources.

- Every factual claim is supported by an approved reference.

**Theoretical foundation**

- **Extraneous material harms learning.** “People learn more deeply from a multimedia message when extraneous material is excluded rather than included” (Mayer, 2014, p. 63). Interesting but irrelevant additions, known as seductive details, tend to reduce learning (Rey, 2012; paraphrased).

- **Why it harms.** “Incidental processing refers to cognitive processes that are not required for making sense of the presented material but are primed by the design of the learning task” (Mayer & Moreno, 2003, p. 45). Extraneous load “can be changed by changing instructional procedures” (Sweller et al., 2019, p. 264); removing unneeded content is the simplest such change.

- **Less is more.** “Only present small amounts of new material at any time, and then assist students as they practice this material” (Rosenshine, 2012, p. 13).

- **Unsupported content undermines validity.** A qualification decision depends on learners being taught and tested on the correct standard (see criterion 2.2 and Foundation 3).

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** Working memory and learner time are both limited. Every unnecessary item competes with essential content and adds seat time and maintenance burden. Unsupported content is worse than unnecessary: it can teach errors. “Relevant to the outcomes” is the test, and the EO is the measuring stick.

**Strength of evidence.** Strong for the effect of extraneous material on learning; policy-based for efficiency.

**Reviewer prompts**

- Which EO does this content serve? If none, why is it here?

- Would removing it change any assessment result?

- What approved reference supports each fact?

**Model note.** *The storyboard removed three background screens that served no EO and moved the system history to an optional job aid. Reuse as an example of content necessity (Mayer, 2014; Rey, 2012).*

## Domain 2: Outcomes, Content, and Alignment

Domain 2 checks the backbone of the design: clear objectives, correct and traceable content, and alignment of everything else to those objectives. It expresses the CFITES *Systems Thinking* and *Systematic Process* principles (National Defence, 2025b, para. 4.11). Alignment is also where instructional design and assessment validity meet: a misaligned assessment cannot support the qualification decision.

### 2.1 Objective quality

**Criterion.** Objectives are observable and measurable and, where applicable, identify performance, conditions, and standards.

**Suggested revision.** “Objectives state an observable performance, the conditions and the standard (PO/EO format), at a cognitive or performance level that matches the job requirement.”

**CFITES link.** Analysis (POs in the QS) and Design (EOs in the TP). A PO is “a statement of the job performance standards to be met and the conditions under which they are performed” (National Defence, 2025a, para. 2). A cadet qualification standard modelled on the A-P9-050 series states that POs “include a description, in performance terms, of what the individual must do, the conditions under which the performance must be completed, and the standard to which the performance must conform”, and that EOs have the same three parts (Cadets and Junior Canadian Rangers Group, 2024, Ch. 4, paras. 2–4).

**What good practice looks like**

- Each objective uses an observable verb (*identify, complete, diagnose, prepare*) rather than *know*, *understand* or *be aware of*.

- Conditions state what the learner is given or denied: references, tools, scenario, time.

- The standard states how well: accuracy, completeness, time, or compliance with a reference.

- For attitude objectives, observable indicators are named (for example, “chooses to report the near-miss”).

**Theoretical foundation**

- **Mager’s three-part objective.** Mager’s widely adopted model holds that a useful objective describes the performance the learner will exhibit, the important conditions under which it will occur, and, wherever possible, the criterion of acceptable performance (Mager, 1997; paraphrased). The CFITES performance–conditions–standard structure follows this model.

- **Outcomes first.** “Constructive alignment (CA) is an outcomes-based approach to teaching in which the learning outcomes that students are intended to achieve are defined before teaching takes place” (Biggs, 2014, p. 5).

- **Cognitive level.** The revised Bloom’s taxonomy classifies objectives by knowledge type and cognitive process, so that objectives, activities and assessments can be compared (Krathwohl, 2002, p. 218).

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** An observable objective tells the designer what to teach, the learner what is expected, and the assessor what to check. Without conditions and a standard, two assessors can reach different decisions about the same performance. The objective is therefore the starting point for both alignment (2.3) and assessment validity (4.2).

**Strength of evidence.** Moderate and policy-based. The performance–conditions–standard structure is CFITES doctrine and long-standing professional consensus; its value comes mainly from enabling alignment and consistent assessment.

**Reviewer prompts**

- Could two assessors agree whether a learner met this objective?

- Are the conditions those of the job?

- Does the verb match the level the job requires?

**Model note.** *EO 402.03: “Given a completed defect report and the relevant CFTO, identify the required corrective action, with no errors in safety-critical steps.” Reuse as a model of a performance–conditions–standard objective (Mager, 1997; National Defence, 2025a).*

### 2.2 Content accuracy and traceability

**Criterion.** Content is accurate, consistent with approved sources, and connected to the intended objectives.

**Suggested revision.** “…accurate, current, consistent with cited approved references, and traceable from QS/PO to EO, teaching point, screen and assessment item.”

**CFITES link.** Design and Development. The CFITES chain is explicit: “This TP describes the Enabling Objectives and teaching points that are derived from the Performance Objectives set out in the QS” (Canadian Defence Academy, 2013, Foreword, para. 2). Control documents are maintained “in both OLs” (National Defence, 2025b, para. 5.8).

**What good practice looks like**

- A traceability matrix links each PO to EOs, teaching points, screens and assessment items.

- Each screen cites its authority (for example, CFTO, doctrine publication, SOP), with edition or date.

- Terminology is consistent across screens, both languages and the assessment.

- An SME has signed off accuracy against the cited references.

**Foundation**

- **Systems logic.** CFITES derives each product from the one before it (QS to TP to materials). Traceability is the evidence that this derivation actually happened.

- **Content evidence for validity.** Standard 4.12: “Test developers should document the extent to which the content domain of a test represents the domain defined in the test specifications” (AERA et al., 2014, p. 89). The same logic applies to instruction: documented traceability shows that what is taught represents what the QS requires.

- **Expert review.** Subject-matter expert review is the first stage of formative evaluation (Tessmer, 1993; paraphrased; see criterion 6.1).

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** Inaccurate content teaches errors, and in safety-critical military tasks errors carry real risk. Traceability lets reviewers find gaps (an EO with no content), excess (content with no EO) and the impact of changes (which screens must change when a CFTO is amended).

**Strength of evidence.** Policy- and practice-based (CFITES systematic process; testing standards on content evidence).

**Reviewer prompts**

- Can each screen be traced to an EO and an approved reference?

- Are the references current?

- Is terminology identical in content, assessment and both languages?

**Model note.** *The traceability matrix links PO 405 to five EOs, 31 screens and 24 items, and each screen footer cites the CFTO paragraph. Reuse as a traceability model (Canadian Defence Academy, 2013; AERA et al., 2014, Standard 4.12).*

### 2.3 Constructive alignment

**Criterion.** Objectives, content, activities, practice, feedback, and assessment work together coherently.

**Note on the term.** Biggs’s “constructive alignment” comes from university teaching and assumes learners construct meaning through activity. In a CFITES context, “instructional alignment” (Cohen, 1987) describes the same check without that theoretical commitment. Either label is defensible; the criterion itself is sound.

**CFITES link.** Design: the TP aligns EOs, teaching points, methods and Performance Checks. The *Systems Thinking* principle (National Defence, 2025b, para. 4.11).

**What good practice looks like**

- For each EO, a reviewer can follow one thread: objective, content, demonstration, practice at the same level, feedback, assessment at the same level.

- Practice activities rehearse what the assessment will require, under similar conditions.

- No assessment item tests content that was not taught or practised.

**Theoretical foundation**

- **Constructive alignment.** Learning outcomes are defined first, and “Teaching and assessment methods are then designed to best achieve those outcomes and to assess the standard at which they have been achieved” (Biggs, 2014, p. 5). “The term ‘alignment’ is used because both teaching and assessment need to be aligned to the intended learning outcomes” (Biggs, 2014, p. 9). Biggs adds that constructive alignment goes further than criterion-referenced assessment “by aligning teaching methods, as well as assessment, to the intended learning outcomes” (Biggs, 2014, p. 9).

- **Instructional alignment.** Cohen reported: “When critical features of instructional stimuli match those of assessment, effect sizes routinely reach 1.2 to 3 sigma” (Cohen, 1987, p. 16). These are large effects from a small set of studies; treat them as showing the importance of alignment rather than as a precise estimate.

- **First Principles.** Merrill warned that “If the application is inconsistent with the intended goals of the instruction, then it will be ineffective” (Merrill, 2002, p. 49), and that “Learning is promoted when the demonstration is consistent with the learning goal” (Merrill, 2002, p. 47).

- **Alignment in assessment.** Alignment is “The degree to which the content and cognitive demands of test questions match targeted content and cognitive demands described in the test specifications” (AERA et al., 2014, p. 216).

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** Alignment is the single check that ties the whole design together. If practice is at a lower level than the assessment, learners are tested on something they never rehearsed; if the assessment is lower than the objective, a pass does not mean the objective was met. Alignment is also the main design-time evidence for the content validity of the Performance Check.

**Strength of evidence.** Strong in principle and widely endorsed; the size of Cohen’s reported effects should not be over-stated.

**Reviewer prompts**

- For one EO, follow the thread from objective to assessment. Is the level the same throughout?

- Does practice rehearse what the assessment requires?

- Is anything assessed that was not taught?

**Model note.** *EO 3 is taught with a worked example, practised in two branching scenarios, and assessed by a similar scenario at the same level, with feedback tied to the EO standard. Reuse as an alignment model (Biggs, 2014; Merrill, 2002).*

### 2.4 Instructional sufficiency \[Description partly reconstructed\]

**Criterion (visible text).** All essential knowledge, skills, and attitudes are addressed…

**Suggested completion.** “…to the depth needed for learners to meet each objective’s standard, including the supporting information, step-by-step guidance and practice that the performance requires.”

**CFITES link.** Analysis and Design. CFITES defines IT&E in terms of “the competencies, skills, knowledge and attitudes a CAF member requires to perform assigned tasks” (National Defence, 2025b, para. 2).

**What good practice looks like**

- A task or content analysis shows the knowledge, skills and attitudes behind each PO, and each one is covered.

- Each EO includes the “why” (how the system works, why a step matters), the “how” (step-by-step procedure), and practice.

- Prerequisite skills are taught or confirmed before the skills that depend on them.

- Attitude components are addressed through rationale, consequences, modelling and scenario choices, not just a statement.

**Theoretical foundation**

- **Different capabilities, different conditions.** Gagné classified learned capabilities into five categories (verbal information, intellectual skills, cognitive strategies, attitudes and motor skills) and argued that each differs in the conditions most favourable for its learning (Gagné, 1985; paraphrased). Sufficiency must therefore be judged for each type of outcome, not for “content” in general.

- **Learning hierarchies.** Gagné’s learning-hierarchy work showed that complex intellectual skills depend on prerequisite skills, which must be learned first (Gagné, 1968; paraphrased).

- **Four components of complex learning.** The four-component instructional design model specifies learning tasks, supportive information, procedural information and part-task practice (van Merriënboer & Kirschner, 2018; paraphrased). Missing any component leaves a predictable gap.

- **All phases of instruction.** Merrill observed that “Much instructional practice concentrates primarily on the demonstration phase and ignores the other phases in this cycle of learning” (Merrill, 2002, p. 44).

- **The instructional equivalent of construct underrepresentation.** Just as a test can fail “to capture important aspects of the construct” (AERA et al., 2014, p. 12), instruction can omit an essential component of the competence. This parallel is this guide’s interpretation.

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** Sufficiency is the counterpart of content necessity (1.3): 1.3 removes what is not needed, 2.4 ensures nothing needed is missing. The most common gaps in e-learning are supporting “why” knowledge, practice, and the attitude component, all of which affect performance on the job.

**Strength of evidence.** Moderate (instructional-design theory, with empirical support for prerequisite sequencing and four-component design).

**Reviewer prompts**

- Which knowledge, skills and attitudes does each PO require, and where is each addressed?

- Does each EO include both explanation and practice?

- Are prerequisites covered before they are needed?

**Model note.** *Each EO in Module 5 contains a short “how it works” explanation, a step-by-step job aid, guided practice and a scenario showing the consequences of skipping the safety check (attitude). Reuse as a model of instructional sufficiency (Gagné, 1985; van Merriënboer & Kirschner, 2018).*

## Domain 3: Learning Strategy and Experience

Domain 3 checks how learning happens: whether methods suit the outcome, the sequence builds competence step by step, learners see good performance, actively think and practise, and practise in conditions like the job. This domain has the deepest research base in the guide. Merrill’s First Principles of Instruction (2002) are a useful single framework for it: problem-centred, activation, demonstration, application, integration.

### 3.1 Method–outcome fit

**Criterion.** Instructional methods match the type and level of learning required.

**CFITES link.** Design (selection of instructional methods for each EO in the TP).

**What good practice looks like**

| Type of learning outcome      | Methods that fit                                                   | Methods that do not fit on their own |
|-------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| Facts and terminology         | Presentation with retrieval practice                               | Long unguided exploration            |
| Concepts (classifying)        | Examples and non-examples, then classification practice            | Definitions only                     |
| Procedures                    | Step-by-step demonstration, then guided and independent practice   | Reading a list of steps              |
| Processes and principles      | Visualizations, explanations of cause and effect, prediction tasks | Unexplained animation                |
| Decisions and problem solving | Worked examples, then scenarios with consequences and feedback     | Recall quizzes                       |
| Attitudes and behaviour       | Modelling, consequences, choices in realistic scenarios            | Statements of policy                 |

**Theoretical foundation**

- **Conditions of learning.** Gagné argued that different categories of learned capability (verbal information, intellectual skills, cognitive strategies, attitudes, motor skills) differ not only in the performances they make possible but also in the conditions most favourable for their learning (Gagné, 1985; paraphrased).

- **Demonstration matched to the goal.** “Learning is promoted when the demonstration is consistent with the learning goal: (a) examples and nonexamples for concepts, (b) demonstrations for procedures, (c) visualizations for processes, and (d) modeling for behavior” (Merrill, 2002, p. 47).

- **Guidance for novices.** “Minimally guided instruction is less effective and less efficient than instructional approaches that place a strong emphasis on guidance of the student learning process. The advantage of guidance begins to recede only when learners have sufficiently high prior knowledge to provide ‘internal’ guidance” (Kirschner et al., 2006, p. 75).

- **Methods, not media.** The delivery medium does not by itself produce learning; the instructional method does (Clark, 1983; see criterion 5.3).

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** There is no single best method. A page-turner can teach terminology but cannot teach a decision; an open simulation can teach an expert but overwhelm a novice. Method–outcome fit is judged against the objective’s verb (2.1) and the learners’ experience (1.2).

**Strength of evidence.** Strong (conditions-of-learning theory, First Principles synthesis, guidance research).

**Reviewer prompts**

- What type of outcome is each EO, and does the method suit that type?

- Is the level of guidance right for these learners?

- Is any outcome taught by “telling” when it requires doing?

**Model note.** *The concept EO uses six examples and four near-miss non-examples before a classification exercise; the procedure EO uses a step-by-step demonstration and guided practice. Reuse as a model of method–outcome fit (Gagné, 1985; Merrill, 2002).*

### 3.2 Sequence and progression

**Criterion.** Learning progresses logically from prerequisite knowledge to more complex or independent performance.

**CFITES link.** Design (sequencing of EOs and teaching points in the TP).

**What good practice looks like**

- Modules open by activating what learners already know.

- Prerequisites come before the skills that depend on them.

- Tasks move from simple to complex and from guided to independent.

- Key terms and components are taught before the procedure that uses them.

**Theoretical foundation**

- **Activation.** “Learning is promoted when relevant previous experience is activated” (Merrill, 2002, p. 46).

- **Small steps.** “Only present small amounts of new material at any time, and then assist students as they practice this material” (Rosenshine, 2012, p. 13).

- **Prerequisites and simple-to-complex.** Learning-hierarchy research holds that complex skills build on prerequisite skills (Gagné, 1968; paraphrased). Elaboration theory sequences instruction from a simple overview to progressively more detailed and complex content (Reigeluth & Stein, 1983; paraphrased). The four-component model organizes whole tasks into classes that run from simple to complex, with support reduced within each class (van Merriënboer & Kirschner, 2018; paraphrased).

- **Pre-training.** “People learn more deeply from a multimedia message when they have learned the names and characteristics of the main concepts” (Mayer, 2014, p. 65).

- **Guidance fades with expertise.** Guidance “can be relaxed only with increased expertise as knowledge in long-term memory can take over from external guidance” (Kirschner et al., 2006, p. 80).

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** Sequencing manages intrinsic cognitive load: each new step builds on knowledge already in long-term memory, so working memory is not overwhelmed. Progression from guided to independent performance is what turns knowledge into the unaided performance the PO requires.

**Strength of evidence.** Strong for small steps, pre-training and guidance fading; moderate for specific sequencing models.

**Reviewer prompts**

- Does any screen use a term or skill that has not yet been taught?

- Does the module end with learners performing more independently than at the start?

**Model note.** *Lesson 4 opens with a recall of the inspection basics, pre-teaches component names, then moves from a fully guided inspection to an unaided one. Reuse as a model of sequence and progression (Merrill, 2002; Rosenshine, 2012).*

### 3.3 Demonstration and modelling

**Criterion.** Accurate examples or demonstrations show learners what effective performance looks like.

**Suggested revision.** Add: “…including, where useful, worked examples and contrasting examples of common errors.”

**CFITES link.** Design and Development (instructional strategy for each EO).

**What good practice looks like**

- Procedures are shown step by step, with the reasoning behind each step made visible.

- Decisions are modelled with a worked example that explains why each choice was made.

- Behaviours are modelled in realistic scenes, sometimes with a contrasting poor example.

- The demonstration meets the same standard the learner will be held to.

**Theoretical foundation**

- **Demonstration principle.** “Principle 3—Demonstration (Show me): Learning is promoted when the instruction demonstrates what is to be learned rather than merely telling information about what is to be learned” (Merrill, 2002, p. 47).

- **Cognitive apprenticeship.** “Modelling involves showing an expert carrying out a task so that students can observe and build a conceptual model of the processes that are required to accomplish the task” (Collins et al., 1987, pp. 16–17).

- **Worked examples.** “Worked examples are instructional devices that provide an expert’s problem solution for a learner to study” (Atkinson et al., 2000, p. 181). “Providing students with models and worked examples can help them learn to solve problems faster” (Rosenshine, 2012, p. 15).

- **Observational learning.** Bandura argued that learning would be laborious and hazardous if people relied only on the results of their own actions, and that most human behaviour is learned by observing models (Bandura, 1977; paraphrased).

- **Behaviour modelling training.** A meta-analysis found that behaviour modelling training effects “were largest for learning outcomes, smaller for job behavior, and smaller still for results outcomes”, and that “Transfer was greatest when mixed (negative and positive) models were presented, when practice included trainee-generated scenarios, when trainees were instructed to set goals” (Taylor et al., 2005, p. 692).

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** For novices, studying a demonstration or worked example is more efficient than discovering the performance by trial and error, because it frees working memory to build an accurate mental model. Showing both good and poor performance helps learners recognize the standard. The demonstration must be accurate: learners copy what they see.

**Strength of evidence.** Strong (worked-example research, behaviour-modelling meta-analysis).

**Reviewer prompts**

- Does every procedure and decision have a demonstration before learners must perform it?

- Is the expert’s reasoning made visible, not just the actions?

- Does the demonstration meet the standard?

**Model note.** *Screen 2.6 shows a narrated walkthrough of a correctly completed inspection, then a contrasting example with two common errors highlighted and explained. Reuse as a model of demonstration with mixed models (Merrill, 2002; Taylor et al., 2005).*

### 3.4 Meaningful participation

**Criterion.** Interactions require purposeful thinking, decision-making, application, or performance rather than decorative selection.

**CFITES link.** Design and Development (interaction design).

**What good practice looks like**

- Interactions ask learners to decide, predict, classify, explain or perform, not just click to reveal.

- Each interaction maps to an EO.

- Learners generate something (an answer, an explanation, a choice with consequences) before seeing the expert answer.

**Theoretical foundation**

- **Cognitive, not just behavioural, activity.** Mayer concluded that “the constructivist view of learning may be best supported by methods of instruction that involve cognitive activity rather than behavioral activity, instructional guidance rather than pure discovery, and curricular focus rather than unstructured exploration” (Mayer, 2004, p. 14).

- **ICAP framework.** “The ICAP hypothesis predicts that as students become more engaged with the learning materials, from passive to active to constructive to interactive, their learning will increase” (Chi & Wylie, 2014, p. 219).

- **Application principle.** “Principle 4—Application (Let me): Learning is promoted when learners are required to use their new knowledge or skill to solve problems” (Merrill, 2002, p. 49).

- **Generative processing.** Generative processing “involves making sense of the material by reorganizing it into a coherent structure and integrating it with relevant prior knowledge” (Mayer, 2014, p. 61). Generative learning strategies include summarizing, mapping, self-testing, self-explaining and enacting (Fiorella & Mayer, 2016; paraphrased).

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** Clicking is not thinking. A click-to-reveal tab is behaviourally active but cognitively passive. The criterion’s phrase “rather than decorative selection” is exactly the cognitive-versus-behavioural distinction in the research. Interactions earn their development cost when they make learners generate, decide or apply.

**Strength of evidence.** Strong (ICAP research, generative-learning research, guided-method reviews).

**Reviewer prompts**

- What does the learner have to think about to complete this interaction?

- Could the learner complete it without understanding the content?

- In ICAP terms, is it passive, active, constructive or interactive?

**Model note.** *Instead of click-to-reveal tabs, Screen 3.2 asks learners to predict the system’s response to a fault before showing the result and explanation. Reuse as a model of constructive participation (Chi & Wylie, 2014; Mayer, 2004).*

### 3.5 Practice and scaffolding

**Criterion.** Practice is distributed through the learning, supported initially, and becomes more independent over time.

**Suggested revision.** The criterion combines two mechanisms; name both: “Practice includes retrieval and is spaced across the learning (and after it, where possible); support is provided at first and faded until learners perform independently to the standard.”

**CFITES link.** Design (practice opportunities and formative checks in the TP).

**What good practice looks like**

- Learners recall and apply earlier content in later modules (spaced retrieval), not only right after it is taught.

- Early practice offers hints, partial worked examples or job aids; later practice removes them.

- The final practice resembles the Performance Check, without support.

- Practice includes feedback (see 4.4).

**Theoretical foundation**

- **Practice testing and spacing.** “Practice testing and distributed practice received high utility assessments because they benefit learners of different ages and abilities and have been shown to boost students’ performance across many criterion tasks and even in educational contexts” (Dunlosky et al., 2013, p. 5). Distributed practice “works across students of different ages, with a wide variety of materials, on the majority of standard laboratory measures, and over long delays” (Dunlosky et al., 2013, p. 39).

- **Spacing meta-analysis.** “Spaced (vs. massed) learning of items consistently shows benefits, regardless of retention interval, and learning benefits increase with increased time lags between learning presentations” (Cepeda et al., 2006, p. 371). This review covered verbal recall tasks.

- **Testing as learning.** “Testing is a powerful means of improving learning, not just assessing it” (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006, p. 249).

- **Scaffolding and fading.** “A scaffold is a temporary support that is used to assist a learner. These scaffolds are gradually withdrawn as learners become more competent” (Rosenshine, 2012, p. 18). In cognitive apprenticeship, “Fading consists of the gradual removal of supports until students are on their own” (Collins et al., 1987, pp. 16–17). Merrill states that learning is promoted when learners are coached “and when this coaching is gradually withdrawn” (Merrill, 2002, p. 49). The term “scaffolding” was introduced by Wood et al. (1976) to describe a tutor controlling the parts of a task beyond the learner’s current capacity (paraphrased).

- **Independent practice.** “Students need extensive, successful, independent practice in order for skills and knowledge to become automatic” (Rosenshine, 2012, p. 18).

- **Deliberate practice.** “In the absence of adequate feedback, efficient learning is impossible and improvement only minimal even for highly motivated subjects” (Ericsson et al., 1993, p. 367).

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** Spaced retrieval makes learning last; scaffolding makes early practice possible for novices; fading makes the final performance independent, as the PO requires. A module that teaches everything once and tests it immediately produces short-term performance that fades before learners reach the job.

**Strength of evidence.** Strong (practice testing and distributed practice are among the best-supported techniques in learning research).

**Reviewer prompts**

- Is earlier content retrieved again later in the course?

- Where are supports provided, and where are they removed?

- Is there unaided practice at the standard before the Performance Check?

**Model note.** *Each module opens with three retrieval questions on earlier modules; scenario practice moves from hinted, to job-aid-supported, to unaided. Reuse as a model of spaced retrieval and fading (Dunlosky et al., 2013; Rosenshine, 2012).*

### 3.6 Authenticity and transfer

**Criterion.** Scenarios, terminology, tools, and decisions resemble the intended workplace or operational context.

**Suggested revision.** “…reflect the cognitive and functional demands of the intended workplace or operational context, with a level of fidelity matched to the learning need.” This avoids implying that maximum realism is always required.

**CFITES link.** Analysis (job context in the QS) and Validation, which checks whether training “has prepared individuals to perform their jobs” (National Defence, 2015, para. 7).

**What good practice looks like**

- Scenarios use real CAF situations, equipment names, forms and terminology.

- Decisions are those members actually face, with realistic consequences.

- The fidelity chosen fits the learning need: a decision task needs realistic information and consequences more than photorealistic graphics.

- Learners finish by applying the skill to their own job context.

**Theoretical foundation**

- **Transfer defined.** “Positive transfer of training is defined as the degree to which trainees effectively apply the knowledge, skills, and attitudes gained in a training context to the job” (Baldwin & Ford, 1988, p. 63). “For transfer to have occurred, learned behavior must be generalized to the job context and maintained over a period of time on the job” (Baldwin & Ford, 1988, p. 63).

- **Training design drives transfer.** A review of transfer research identified “training design (behavioral modeling, error management, realistic training environments)” among the factors with “the strongest, most consistent relationships with the transfer of training” (Grossman & Salas, 2011, p. 103).

- **Situated cognition.** “Knowledge is situated, being in part a product of the activity, context, and culture in which it is developed and used” (Brown et al., 1989, p. 32).

- **Problem-centred and integration principles.** “Learning is promoted when learners are engaged in solving real-world problems” (Merrill, 2002, p. 45). “Learning is promoted when learners are encouraged to integrate (transfer) the new knowledge or skill into their everyday life” (Merrill, 2002, p. 50).

- **Fidelity.** “Physical fidelity is defined as the degree to which the physical simulation looks, sounds, and feels like the operational environment”; “Functional fidelity is defined as the degree to which the simulation acts like the operational equipment in reacting to the tasks executed by the trainee” (Alexander et al., 2005, p. 4). “There is a relationship between fidelity and transfer, but it is not as simple as ‘more is better’” (Alexander et al., 2005, p. 6).

- **Open versus closed skills.** A transfer meta-analysis found that predictors such as motivation and work environment were more strongly related to transfer for open skills (for example, leadership) than for closed skills (for example, software use) (Blume et al., 2010, p. 1065; paraphrased).

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** People apply knowledge most readily in situations that resemble where they learned it. Authentic scenarios also support the extrapolation inference in the validity argument (Foundation 3): a learner who succeeds in a realistic scenario gives more plausible evidence of job readiness than one who succeeds on abstract recall items. The research on fidelity cautions that realism should target the features that matter for the task, not cosmetic detail.

**Strength of evidence.** Strong for the link between training design and transfer; moderate for specific fidelity levels.

**Reviewer prompts**

- Would a member of this trade recognize the scenario as real?

- Are the decisions and consequences those of the job?

- Is the level of realism spent where it matters for the task?

**Model note.** *The capstone scenario uses an actual defect-report form, current equipment names and a time-pressured decision with realistic consequences; graphics are simple line drawings. Reuse as a model of functional fidelity (Alexander et al., 2005; Grossman & Salas, 2011).*

## Domain 4: Assessment, Feedback, and Mastery

Domain 4 checks whether assessment results can be trusted and whether learners get what they need to reach the standard. The anchor is the validity frame in Foundation 3: each criterion either protects the meaning of the score or closes the gap between current and required performance. Feedback and formative assessment are among the best-evidenced practices in this guide.

### 4.1 Assessment purpose

**Criterion.** Formative practice and summative assessment have clear and distinct purposes.

**Suggested revision.** Add: “…and each result is used for its purpose: formative results guide the learner’s next steps; summative results support the PO or EO achievement decision.”

**CFITES link.** Design (Performance Checks and formative checks in the TP) and Evaluation of Learners (Vol. 7). CAF documents use exactly this distinction: “Formative testing helps the learner and the instructor recognize progress or lapses in learning, so that confirmation or corrective action can be provided”, while summative testing “Determines whether candidates have achieved Performance Objectives (POs) or critical Enabling Objectives (EOs)” (National Defence, 2009a, paras. 6.1–6.2). A CAF training plan states that learners are “subject to an ongoing process of evaluation, both formative and summative” (Canadian Defence Academy, 2013, Ch. 3, para. 1).

**What good practice looks like**

- Knowledge checks and practice items are labelled as practice, allow multiple attempts, and give hints and explanations.

- The Performance Check is clearly separate, standardized and secure, with no hints, and is scored against the PO standard.

- Formative results are not added into the pass/fail decision.

- The storyboard states the purpose of each assessment event.

**Theoretical foundation**

- **Origin of the distinction.** Scriven introduced the terms in programme evaluation: “this kind of terminal evaluation (we might call it summative as opposed to formative evaluation)” (Scriven, 1966, p. 5). Scriven later quoted Robert Stake’s summary: “When the cook tastes the soup, that’s formative; when the guests taste the soup, that’s summative” (Scriven, 1981, p. 63).

- **Applied to learners.** Bloom carried the idea into instruction: “We have borrowed the term Formative Evaluation from Scriven (1967)” (Bloom, 1968, p. 9).

- **Formative assessment defined.** Black and Wiliam’s review defined formative assessment as “encompassing all those activities undertaken by teachers, and/or by their students, which provide information to be used as feedback to modify the teaching and learning activities in which they are engaged” (Black & Wiliam, 1998, pp. 7–8). Their later theory adds that practice is formative “to the extent that evidence about student achievement is elicited, interpreted, and used … to make decisions about the next steps in instruction” (Black & Wiliam, 2009, p. 9).

- **Purpose drives validity evidence.** Standard 1.0: “Clear articulation of each intended test score interpretation for a specified use should be set forth, and appropriate validity evidence in support of each intended interpretation should be provided” (AERA et al., 2014, p. 23).

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** Purpose determines design. Formative items can be low-stakes, hinted and repeatable because their job is to inform the next step. Summative items must be standardized and secure because their job is to support a qualification decision. Mixing the two damages both: hints in a Performance Check inflate scores (construct-irrelevant easiness), and grading practice discourages learners from risking errors they could learn from.

**Strength of evidence.** Strong conceptual and professional consensus; the size of formative-assessment effects varies across studies.

**Reviewer prompts**

- Can the learner tell which activities are practice and which count?

- Do any summative items give hints, feedback during the test, or unlimited attempts on the same item?

- Is each formative result used to direct the learner somewhere?

**Model note.** *Each EO ends with an ungraded knowledge check with explanatory feedback; the PO is assessed by a separate, secure scenario-based PC drawn from an item pool. Reuse as a model of distinct formative and summative purposes (Black & Wiliam, 1998; AERA et al., 2014, Standard 1.0).*

### 4.2 Assessment alignment and validity \[Reconstructed\]

**Criterion (reconstructed).** Assessment items and tasks measure the stated objectives at the intended cognitive or performance level.

**CFITES link.** Analysis and Design. A PO states the job performance, the conditions and the standard (National Defence, 2025a, para. 2), and summative tests determine whether learners “have achieved Performance Objectives (POs) or critical Enabling Objectives (EOs)” (National Defence, 2009a, para. 6.2). The check must therefore match the PO’s performance, conditions and standard.

**What good practice looks like**

- An assessment blueprint (table of specifications) maps every item or task to an EO or PO.

- Item verbs match objective verbs: an objective to *apply* or *perform* is assessed with application or performance, not recall.

- Conditions in the test match the objective’s conditions (references allowed, tools, time, cues).

- Where e-learning can assess only the knowledge component of a PO, the blueprint states where the performance itself is assessed (for example, a practical PC).

**Theoretical foundation**

- **Alignment defined.** “The degree to which the content and cognitive demands of test questions match targeted content and cognitive demands described in the test specifications” (AERA et al., 2014, p. 216). Webb’s alignment research uses a similar definition: “Alignment is the degree to which expectations and assessments are in agreement and serve in conjunction with one another to guide the system toward students learning what they are expected to know and do” (Webb, 1997, p. 4). Webb’s criteria include depth-of-knowledge consistency (Webb, 1997, p. 15).

- **Content representation.** Standard 4.12: “Test developers should document the extent to which the content domain of a test represents the domain defined in the test specifications” (AERA et al., 2014, p. 89). For educational tests, analyses “should make explicit those aspects of the target domain that the test represents, as well as those aspects that the test fails to represent” (AERA et al., 2014, p. 196, Standard 12.4).

- **Cognitive level.** The revised Bloom’s taxonomy provides a tool for this check: “Using the Table to classify objectives, activities, and assessments provides a clear, concise, visual representation of a particular course or unit. Once completed, the entries in the Taxonomy Table can be used to examine relative emphasis, curriculum alignment, and missed educational opportunities” (Krathwohl, 2002, p. 218).

- **Construct underrepresentation.** An assessment that is “too narrow and fails to include important dimensions or facets of the construct” (Messick, 1994, p. 4) cannot support a claim about the whole objective.

- **Ambitious claims need more evidence.** “More-ambitious claims require more support than less-ambitious claims” (Kane, 2013, p. 1). A claim that a learner can *perform* a task needs stronger evidence than a claim that they can *recall* it.

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** Alignment must hold on three dimensions: content (the same knowledge or skill), cognitive or performance level (the same process), and conditions and standard (as stated in the PO or EO). A multiple-choice recall test of a performance objective underrepresents the construct, so a pass cannot plausibly support the claim “qualified to perform”. The blueprint is the evidence that alignment was designed, not assumed.

**Strength of evidence.** Strong; this is a core requirement of the professional testing standards.

**Reviewer prompts**

- Does every item trace to an EO or PO in the blueprint?

- Does the item ask learners to do what the objective verb says, under its conditions?

- Which parts of the PO does this assessment not cover, and where are they assessed?

**Model note.** *The PC blueprint maps 20 items to EOs 401.01–401.05, weighted by criticality; “apply” EOs are assessed with scenario items, not definitions. Reuse as a model of content and cognitive alignment (AERA et al., 2014, Standard 4.12; Krathwohl, 2002).*

### 4.3 Fairness and clarity

**Criterion.** Instructions, response options, criteria, and expected standards are clear and free from irrelevant difficulty.

**Suggested revision.** “…clear, accessible in either official language, and free from construct-irrelevant difficulty **and clues** (unnecessary reading load, trick wording, confusing interfaces, implausible distractors).” The original wording covers irrelevant difficulty but not irrelevant easiness.

**CFITES link.** Design and Development of Performance Checks and their marking guides; Evaluation of Learners (Vol. 7).

**What good practice looks like**

- Instructions state exactly what to do, how responses are scored, and what standard is required.

- Stems are clear and concise; vocabulary suits the learners; no trick or double-negative items.

- Distractors are plausible and based on typical errors; there are no length, grammar or “all of the above” clues.

- Interaction formats (drag-and-drop, hotspot) are easy to operate and keyboard-accessible.

- Performance criteria or rubrics are shared with learners before the assessment.

**Theoretical foundation**

- **The definition of irrelevant difficulty.** “Construct-irrelevant variance may result from a lack of clarity in test instructions, from unrelated complexity or language demands in test tasks, and/or from other characteristics of test items that are unrelated to the construct” (AERA et al., 2014, p. 55).

- **Difficulty and easiness.** Messick describes construct-irrelevant difficulty: “aspects of the task that are extraneous to the focal construct make the task irrelevantly difficult for some individuals or groups. An example is the intrusion of undue reading-comprehension requirements in a test of subject-matter knowledge” (Messick, 1994, p. 5). The reverse also occurs: “construct-irrelevant easiness occurs when extraneous clues in item or task formats permit some individuals to respond correctly or appropriately in ways irrelevant to the construct being assessed” (Messick, 1994, p. 5).

- **Fairness standard.** Standard 3.0: “All steps in the testing process, including test design, validation, development, administration, and scoring procedures, should be designed in such a manner as to minimize construct-irrelevant variance and to promote valid score interpretations for the intended uses for all examinees in the intended population” (AERA et al., 2014, p. 63). “The central idea of fairness in testing is to identify and remove construct-irrelevant barriers to maximal performance for any examinee” (AERA et al., 2014, p. 63).

- **Universal design of assessment.** The seven elements are “Inclusive assessment population; Precisely defined constructs; Accessible, non-biased items; Amenable to accommodations; Simple, clear, and intuitive instructions and procedures; Maximum readability and comprehensibility; Maximum legibility” (Thompson et al., 2002, p. 6).

- **Technology-enhanced items.** New item formats “should be supported with evidence that the formats are a feasible way to collect information about the construct, that they do not introduce construct-irrelevant variance, and that steps have been taken to promote accessibility for all students” (AERA et al., 2014, p. 197).

- **Item-writing guidelines.** A validated taxonomy of 31 multiple-choice guidelines includes: keep directions in the stem clear, keep vocabulary simple, minimize reading, avoid trick items, make all distractors plausible, and base distractors on learners’ typical errors (Haladyna et al., 2002; paraphrased).

- **Shared criteria.** A review of rubric research concluded that rubrics can make scoring more reliable, especially when analytic and supported by exemplars or rater training, and may support learning by making criteria explicit; rubrics alone do not guarantee validity (Jonsson & Svingby, 2007; paraphrased).

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** “Irrelevant difficulty” is construct-irrelevant variance, the central concept of assessment fairness. The design question is always: is this item hard (or easy) because of the competence, or because of something else? Plausible distractors built from real errors make an item diagnose misunderstanding; implausible ones make it easy for the wrong reason. Clear, shared criteria let learners aim at the standard and let markers apply it consistently.

**Strength of evidence.** Strong for the principle (testing standards, validity theory). Moderate for individual item-writing rules, several of which rest on expert consensus rather than experiments.

**Reviewer prompts**

- Could a competent learner get this wrong because of wording, reading load or the interface?

- Could a non-competent learner get it right through a clue?

- Is the standard for success stated before the assessment?

**Model note.** *Each PC item uses a short scenario stem, three distractors drawn from errors observed in the pilot, and keyboard-operable responses; the marking checklist is shown to learners in the lesson introduction. Reuse as a model of minimizing construct-irrelevant variance (AERA et al., 2014, Standard 3.0; Messick, 1994).*

### 4.4 Feedback quality

**Criterion.** Feedback confirms the result, reinforces or corrects understanding, and identifies the next action.

**Suggested revision.** “Feedback is task-focused and timely; it confirms the result, explains why the response is correct or incorrect, and identifies the next action.” This adds explanation and excludes praise-only feedback.

**CFITES link.** Design and Development of formative checks. The CAF definition of formative testing already includes “confirmation or corrective action” (National Defence, 2009a, para. 6.1).

**What good practice looks like**

- Every response option has its own feedback, not a generic “Incorrect, try again”.

- Feedback explains why, refers to the key concept or step, and points to where to review.

- Feedback is about the task and the reasoning, not the learner (“Great job, you’re a natural!”).

**Theoretical foundation**

- **Definition and purpose.** Feedback is “information provided by an agent (e.g., teacher, peer, book, parent, self, experience) regarding aspects of one’s performance or understanding” (Hattie & Timperley, 2007, p. 81). “The main purpose of feedback is to reduce discrepancies between current understandings and performance and a goal” (Hattie & Timperley, 2007, p. 86).

- **Three questions.** “Effective feedback must answer three major questions asked by a teacher and/or by a student: Where am I going? (What are the goals?), How am I going? (What progress is being made toward the goal?), and Where to next? (What activities need to be undertaken to make better progress?)” (Hattie & Timperley, 2007, p. 86).

- **Formative feedback.** “Formative feedback is defined in this review as information communicated to the learner that is intended to modify his or her thinking or behavior for the purpose of improving learning” (Shute, 2008, p. 154). Shute distinguishes two parts: “Verification is defined as the simple judgment of whether an answer is correct, and elaboration is the informational aspect of the message, providing relevant cues to guide the learner toward a correct answer” (Shute, 2008, p. 158).

- **Computer-based feedback.** A meta-analysis of feedback in computer-based environments found that “elaborated feedback (EF; e.g., providing an explanation) produced larger effect sizes (0.49) than feedback regarding the correctness of the answer (KR; 0.05) or providing the correct answer (KCR; 0.32)”, and that EF was particularly effective “for higher order learning outcomes” (Van der Kleij et al., 2015, p. 475).

- **More information, more effect.** A meta-analysis of 435 studies found “a medium effect (d = 0.48) of feedback on student learning” and that “the impact is substantially influenced by the information content conveyed” (Wisniewski et al., 2020, p. 1). “Feedback is more effective the more information it contains” (Wisniewski et al., 2020, p. 12).

- **Feedback can harm.** Feedback interventions “improved performance on average (d = .41) but … over ⅓ of the FIs decreased performance” (Kluger & DeNisi, 1996, p. 254), and effectiveness “decreases as attention moves up the hierarchy closer to the self and away from the task” (p. 254). “Praise addressed to students is unlikely to be effective, because it carries little information” (Hattie & Timperley, 2007, p. 96).

| Criterion element                                       | Hattie & Timperley question              | Shute component              |
|---------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------|------------------------------|
| Confirms the result                                     | How am I going?                          | Verification                 |
| Reinforces or corrects understanding                    | How am I going? (task and process level) | Elaboration                  |
| Identifies the next action                              | Where to next?                           | Elaboration (guidance)       |
| *Not in current wording:* links to the goal or standard | Where am I going?                        | Elaboration (goal reference) |

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** The criterion’s three parts match the strongest findings: verification plus explanation plus a next step. “Correct” or “Incorrect” alone has almost no effect (0.05 in Van der Kleij et al., 2015); explanatory feedback has the largest effect. Because a third of feedback interventions made performance worse, the presence of feedback is not enough; its content and focus decide whether it helps.

**Strength of evidence.** Strong; several large meta-analyses agree.

**Reviewer prompts**

- Does each option’s feedback explain why, not just whether?

- Does it name the next action or review point?

- Is any feedback directed at the learner’s ability rather than the task?

**Model note.** *Each distractor in the EO 3 knowledge check has option-specific feedback that names the misconception, explains the correct principle and links to the relevant screen. Reuse as a model of elaborated, task-focused feedback (Hattie & Timperley, 2007; Shute, 2008).*

### 4.5 Remediation and mastery

**Criterion.** The design provides appropriate review, retry, or further practice when learners need support.

**Suggested revision.** “…provides targeted correctives linked to the specific objective missed, followed by reassessment on an alternate form or different items.”

**CFITES link.** Conduct (Vol. 6) and Evaluation of Learners. Volume 6 directs that a deficiency “is discussed with the learner to determine if remedial training can solve the problem” (Department of National Defence, 2002, p. 54), and a progress review board may decide on “continuation with remedial training, recourse or cease training” (p. 55). CATO 24-07 permits a retake of a failed PC if the “necessary remedial training, information and counselling is provided” (National Defence, 2009a, para. 11.2). Retest limits are set in the course control document.

**What good practice looks like**

- When a learner misses an objective, the module routes them to review material for that objective, not back to the start.

- Retries use different items or scenarios that test the same objective.

- Retest limits and the escalation path (instructor contact, progress review) are stated.

**Theoretical foundation**

- **Mastery learning.** “Most students (perhaps over 90 percent) can master what we have to teach them, and it is the task of instruction to find the means which will enable our students to master the subject under consideration” (Bloom, 1968, p. 1). Bloom stressed diagnosis: “The diagnosis should be accompanied by a very specific prescription if the students are to do anything about it” (Bloom, 1968, p. 9).

- **Time as the variable.** Carroll restated his model as: “the degree of learning or achievement is a function of the ratio of the time actually spent on learning to the time needed to learn” (Carroll, 1989, p. 26). Mastery designs hold the standard constant and let time and support vary.

- **Correctives and second assessment.** In mastery learning, formative assessment is followed by targeted “correctives”, and a second formative assessment checks whether they worked (Guskey, 2010; paraphrased).

- **Evidence.** A meta-analysis of 108 controlled evaluations found that “mastery learning programs have positive effects on the examination performance of students” and that “The effects appear to be stronger on the weaker students in a class” (Kulik et al., 1990, p. 265). The same review noted that self-paced programmes “often reduce the completion rates in college classes” (p. 265).

- **Alternate forms.** Learners who must demonstrate mastery “should have a reasonable number of opportunities to succeed on alternate forms of the test or be provided with technically sound alternatives” (AERA et al., 2014, p. 197, Standard 12.9).

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** CFITES objectives are criterion-referenced: the standard is fixed and every qualified member must meet it. Mastery learning provides the matching instructional logic, because time and support vary while the standard stays constant. Remediation must be diagnostic and specific, and reassessment must use alternate items. Retrying identical items measures memory of the answer key, a construct-irrelevant source of success that undermines the summative decision.

**Strength of evidence.** Moderate to strong. Mastery learning effects are positive in meta-analysis. Bloom’s (1984) “two-sigma” figure is widely cited but has been publicly questioned, so avoid relying on it.

**Reviewer prompts**

- Does remediation target the objective missed?

- Do retries draw on different items or scenarios?

- Are retest limits and escalation consistent with the course control document?

**Model note.** *Learners who miss EO 2 items are routed to a two-screen review and a worked example, then reassessed with a parallel item set from the pool. Reuse as a model of correctives and alternate-form reassessment (Bloom, 1968; AERA et al., 2014, Standard 12.9).*

## Domain 5: Multimedia, Accessibility, and Development Readiness

Domain 5 checks whether the build will work for real learners on real systems. Media must earn their place, cognitive load must be managed, technology must serve a learning function, every learner must be able to use the product in either official language, and developers must be able to build it without guessing. The evidence is strongest for multimedia and cognitive load; accessibility and official languages rest mainly on law and DND policy.

### 5.1 Multimedia purpose

**Criterion.** Text, narration, visuals, audio, and interactions each make a clear contribution to learning.

**Suggested revision.** Each media element (text, narration, graphic, audio, animation, interaction) has a stated instructional function; decorative or “seductive” elements are removed.

**CFITES link.** Design (method and media selection in the Training Plan) and Development (storyboard and build, Vol. 5).

**What good practice looks like**

- Graphics show something the learner must understand: the equipment, the process, the relationship. Stock imagery that only decorates is absent.

- Narration explains the graphic; it does not read the on-screen text aloud word for word.

- Labels sit on or next to the part of the graphic they describe, and narration plays while the matching part is shown.

- The storyboard states the purpose of each media asset.

**Theoretical foundation**

- **Cognitive theory of multimedia learning.** Mayer’s multimedia principle is: “People learn better from words and pictures than from words alone” (Mayer, 2020, p. 1). The theory’s premise is that “instructional messages should be designed in light of how the human mind works” (Mayer, 2020, p. 7).

- **Three kinds of processing.** Mayer distinguishes “extraneous processing, which drains limited cognitive processing capacity without contributing to learning; essential processing, which involves selecting relevant information and organizing it as presented in working memory; and generative processing, which involves making sense of the material by reorganizing it into a coherent structure and integrating it with relevant prior knowledge” (Mayer, 2014, p. 61). Extraneous processing is “Not related to instructional goal, caused by poor instructional design” (Mayer, 2014, p. 62, Table 1).

- **Coherence principle.** “People learn more deeply from a multimedia message when extraneous material is excluded rather than included” (Mayer, 2014, p. 63). Mayer adds that the effect is strongest “when the extraneous material is highly interesting rather than neutral” (Mayer, 2014, p. 63). An earlier review put it the same way: “Students understand a multimedia explanation better when interesting but extraneous material is excluded rather than included” (Mayer & Moreno, 2003, p. 48).

- **Seductive details.** A meta-analysis of the seductive-detail effect found that interesting but irrelevant additions tend to reduce learning (Rey, 2012; paraphrased).

- **Contiguity principles.** “People learn more deeply from a multimedia message when corresponding printed words and graphics are presented near rather than far from each other on the page or screen” (Mayer, 2014, p. 63). “People learn more deeply from a multimedia message when corresponding graphics and narration are presented simultaneously rather than successively” (Mayer, 2014, p. 64).

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** Media are never neutral. Each element either supports essential and generative processing or drains working memory as extraneous processing. “Clear contribution” is therefore the right test: if the designer cannot name what an element helps the learner understand or do, it is probably extraneous. The multimedia principle supports **relevant** pictures, not pictures for their own sake. Research on picture functions makes the same distinction between decorative pictures and pictures that represent, organize or explain content (Levin et al., 1987; paraphrased).

**Strength of evidence.** Strong. The coherence, contiguity and multimedia principles rest on many replicated experiments.

**Reviewer prompts**

- For each asset: what would the learner lose if it were removed?

- Does narration duplicate long on-screen text word for word?

- Are labels placed on the graphic, or in a separate legend the learner must cross-reference?

**Model note.** *Screen 3.4 uses an annotated cut-away diagram; narration explains each component as it is highlighted, and there is no decorative imagery. Reuse as a model of the coherence and contiguity principles (Mayer, 2014).*

### 5.2 Cognitive manageability

**Criterion.** Content is segmented, paced, and presented without unnecessary duplication or distraction.

**Suggested revision.** Content is presented in learner-paced segments, key terms are introduced before complex material, and presentation avoids redundancy, split attention and distraction, judged against the target learners’ prior knowledge.

**CFITES link.** Design (structure of EOs and teaching points in the TP) and Development (screen design).

**What good practice looks like**

- Each segment carries one main idea and ends with a learner-controlled “Continue”; nothing auto-advances.

- Names and functions of key parts are taught before the procedure that uses them.

- Headings, numbering and highlighting signal the structure.

- Captions and transcripts exist, but full on-screen text is not forced on every learner while narration plays over a complex graphic.

**Theoretical foundation**

- **Cognitive load theory.** “Intrinsic cognitive load referred to the complexity of the information being processed and was related to the concept of element interactivity” (Sweller et al., 2019, pp. 263–264). By contrast, “Extraneous cognitive load is not determined by the intrinsic complexity of the information but rather, how the information is presented and what the learner is required to do by the instructional procedure. Unlike intrinsic cognitive load, it can be changed by changing instructional procedures” (Sweller et al., 2019, p. 264). The design rule follows: “Effective instructional procedures reduce element interactivity while ineffective ones increase element interactivity” (Sweller et al., 2019, p. 264).

- **Load depends on the learner.** “Complexity or element interactivity depends on a combination of both the nature of the information and the knowledge of the person processing the information” (Sweller et al., 2019, p. 264).

- **Segmenting principle.** “People learn more deeply when a multimedia message is presented in learner-paced segments rather than as a continuous unit” (Mayer, 2014, p. 64).

- **Pre-training principle.** “People learn more deeply from a multimedia message when they have learned the names and characteristics of the main concepts” (Mayer, 2014, p. 65).

- **Signaling principle.** “People learn more deeply from a multimedia message when cues are added that highlight the organization of the essential material” (Mayer, 2014, p. 63).

- **Redundancy.** “People learn more deeply from graphics and narration than from graphics, narration, and on-screen text” (Mayer, 2014, p. 63). In the original experiments, when text was both spoken and shown, “the visual form was redundant and imposed a cognitive load that interfered with learning” (Kalyuga et al., 1999, p. 351).

- **Boundary condition.** “The redundancy effect can be diminished or even reversed when the learners are experienced, the on-screen text is short, or the material lacks graphics” (Mayer, 2014, p. 63).

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** Good design manages intrinsic load (segmenting, pre-training, sequencing) and removes extraneous load (redundancy, split attention, distraction). Because load depends on what the learner already knows, “manageable” must be judged for the audience described under criterion 1.2: the same screen can overload a novice and bore an expert.

**Redundancy versus accessibility.** This guide’s reading of the evidence is that the redundancy research concerns text imposed on all learners by default. Captions and transcripts offered as learner-selectable options meet WCAG Success Criterion 1.2.2 without imposing redundancy on everyone. Do not remove captions in the name of the redundancy principle.

**Strength of evidence.** Strong for segmenting, signaling, pre-training and redundancy, with the boundary conditions noted.

**Reviewer prompts**

- Does any screen ask learners to hold several new, interacting elements in mind at once? Could it be split or pre-taught?

- Is pacing under learner control?

- Is anything shown twice in the same channel or across channels without a reason?

**Model note.** *Lesson 2 pre-teaches the six valve components on one labelled screen before the start-up procedure, and each procedure step is a learner-paced segment. Reuse as an example of pre-training and segmenting (Mayer, 2014; Sweller et al., 2019).*

### 5.3 Technology appropriateness

**Criterion.** The selected technology supports demonstration, practice, feedback, interaction, or assessment in a meaningful way.

**Suggested revision.** Add: “…and is justified against simpler alternatives in terms of learning value, access, cost and maintenance.”

**CFITES link.** Design (method and media selection) and the *Optimum Efficiency* principle (National Defence, 2025b, para. 4.11). Volume 6 defines distributed learning as “the delivery of standardized training, education or professional development using multiple media and technologies when and where it is needed” (Department of National Defence, 2002, p. 29). CATO 24-02 calls for “distributed learning (DL) wherever appropriate and practicable” (National Defence, 2015, para. 10).

**What good practice looks like**

- The storyboard says what each technology lets learners do: a simulation for safe practice of a hazardous step, branching for decisions with consequences, automated scoring for immediate feedback.

- A simpler format was considered and rejected for a stated reason.

- The technology runs on the networks and devices learners actually have.

**Theoretical foundation**

- **Methods, not media.** Clark’s review concluded: “Consistent evidence is found for the generalization that there are no learning benefits to be gained from employing any specific medium to deliver instruction” (Clark, 1983, p. 445). His analogy is that media do not influence achievement “any more than the truck that delivers our groceries causes changes in our nutrition” (Clark, 1983, p. 445, as cited in Nathan & Robinson, 2001, p. 71).

- **The counter-argument.** Kozma (1994) argued that some media capabilities, such as simulation and dynamic visualization, make possible methods that other media cannot deliver (paraphrased).

- **Online learning meta-analysis.** The U.S. Department of Education meta-analysis found that “the observed advantage for blended learning conditions is not necessarily rooted in the media used per se and may reflect differences in content, pedagogy and learning time” (Means et al., 2010, p. xv). It also concluded: “When used by itself, online learning appears to be as effective as conventional classroom instruction, but not more so” (Means et al., 2010, p. xviii).

- **Learner-centred design.** “The design of multimedia instructional messages can be based on a technology-centered approach that focuses on the capabilities of advanced technologies, or on a learner-centered approach that focuses on the nature of the human cognitive system” (Mayer, 2020, p. 3).

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** The “meaningful way” test reflects the research consensus that the instructional method, not the delivery technology, produces learning. Kozma’s point refines the test rather than overturning it. A technology is justified when it enables a method that the objective requires, such as realistic practice or immediate feedback, and not because it is new or impressive.

**Strength of evidence.** Strong for “the medium alone does not cause learning”; moderate for the value of specific affordances.

**Reviewer prompts**

- What does this technology let learners do that a simpler format could not?

- Are development and maintenance costs proportionate to that learning value?

- Does it work on DND networks, in low bandwidth, and with assistive technology?

**Model note.** *The branching scenario in Module 4 lets learners make the three triage decisions and see their consequences, which a linear page-turner could not do. Reuse as an example of technology chosen for the practice it enables (Clark, 1983; Means et al., 2010).*

### 5.4 Accessibility and inclusion

**Criterion.** The design anticipates learner variability and includes appropriate alternatives, controls, labels, and navigation.

**Suggested revision.** Add a measurable standard: “…and is designed to conform to WCAG 2.1 Level AA, as incorporated in CAN/ASC-EN 301 549, applying Universal Design for Learning.”

**CFITES link.** Analysis (description of the target population), Design and Development. DAOD 5031-2 lists the Accessible Canada Act among its references (National Defence, 2025b).

**What good practice looks like**

- Every meaningful image has alternative text; decorative images are marked as decorative.

- Videos have captions and a transcript; audio-only content has a transcript.

- Everything works with a keyboard alone, in a logical focus order.

- Time limits can be extended or turned off; moving content can be paused.

- Form fields and buttons have visible labels and programmatic names.

- Key content is offered in more than one form (text, diagram, narration), consistent with UDL.

**Theoretical foundation**

- **Universal Design for Learning.** The current version is UDL Guidelines 3.0, released on 30 July 2024 (CAST, 2024). Its three principles are “Design Multiple Means of Engagement”, “Design Multiple Means of Representation” and “Design Multiple Means of Action & Expression”. The rationale is learner variability: “Learners differ in the ways they perceive and make meaning of information”, so “there is not one means of representation that will be optimal for every learner; providing options for representation is essential” (CAST, 2024, Representation section).

- **WCAG 2.2.** The four principles are: Perceivable, “Information and user interface components must be presentable to users in ways they can perceive”; Operable, “User interface components and navigation must be operable”; Understandable, “Information and the operation of the user interface must be understandable”; and Robust, “Content must be robust enough that it can be interpreted by a wide variety of user agents, including assistive technologies” (World Wide Web Consortium, 2024).

- **Accessible Canada Act.** A barrier is “anything — including anything physical, architectural, technological or attitudinal, anything that is based on information or communications or anything that is the result of a policy or a practice — that hinders the full and equal participation in society of persons with an impairment, including a physical, mental, intellectual, cognitive, learning, communication or sensory impairment or a functional limitation” (Accessible Canada Act, 2019, s. 2). Among its principles: “laws, policies, programs, services and structures must take into account the disabilities of persons, the different ways that persons interact with their environments…” (s. 6(e)). The Act applies to “the Canadian Forces” (s. 7(1)(d)).

- **Current federal ICT requirement.** The Treasury Board *Standard on Web Accessibility* has been rescinded; until new rules apply, departments continue to maintain their public-facing websites and mobile applications “to meet the minimum standard of WCAG 2.0 established in 2011 and updated in 2013” (Rochon, 2026). New regulations define the ICT Standard as “the standard CAN/ASC - EN 301 549, Accessibility requirements for ICT products and services” (Regulations Amending the Accessible Canada Regulations, 2025, s. 19.1). Employee-facing web pages that are created or updated must conform “to the extent feasible, to the applicable requirements set out in clauses 4 to 7 and 9 of the ICT Standard” (s. 19.4(1)). These provisions come into force in December 2027 and December 2028. EN 301 549 states that conformance with “WCAG 2.1” Level AA is equivalent to conforming with its web clauses 9.1 to 9.4 and 9.6 (European Telecommunications Standards Institute, 2021, p. 45).

| 4.1.2 Name, Role, Value      | “…the name and role can be programmatically determined…”                                                                               | Custom interactions announce correctly to screen readers       |

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** UDL supplies the design stance: plan for variability from the start instead of retrofitting. WCAG supplies the testable technical standard. The Accessible Canada Act and its regulations supply the legal duty. Designing to WCAG 2.1 Level AA now prepares products for the regulatory requirement as it comes into force.

**CAF caveat.** The Act states: “Nothing in this Act is to be construed as affecting the principle of universality of service…” (Accessible Canada Act, 2019, s. 9). Universality of service governs who may serve, not whether courseware is accessible. Accessible design still serves members with temporary injuries, learning disabilities or assistive-technology needs, civilian employees, and anyone on a small screen or a slow connection.

**Strength of evidence.** Policy- and standards-based (binding or soon binding). The empirical evidence base for UDL as a framework is still developing, so cite UDL as a design framework and WCAG and the Act as the requirement.

**Reviewer prompts**

- Can the module be completed with keyboard only and with a screen reader?

- Do all media have captions, transcripts or text alternatives?

- Are any time limits essential to the objective? If not, can learners extend them?

**Model note.** *The drag-and-drop sorting activity offers an equivalent keyboard “select and place” mode, and every diagram has alt text describing its instructional point. Reuse as an example of WCAG 2.1.1 and 1.1.1 conformance (World Wide Web Consortium, 2024).*

### 5.5 Official-language quality and equivalence \[Reconstructed\]

**Criterion (reconstructed).** English and French versions are equivalent in content, quality, functionality and assessment difficulty, and both are available when the course is delivered.

**CFITES link.** DAOD 5031-2 makes training authorities responsible for “ensuring IT&E programs are planned and provided to CAF members in the OL of their choice” (National Defence, 2025b, para. 5.7). It also states that programs “shall maintain applicable IT&E control documents in both OLs” (para. 5.8).

**What good practice looks like**

- English and French storyboards are developed in parallel, with the same screens, interactions, feedback and scoring.

- Terminology comes from approved bilingual sources, such as the Defence Terminology Bank and TERMIUM Plus.

- Screen layouts allow for text expansion, so French text is not cut off or shrunk.

- Narration, captions, alt text and assessment items exist in both languages and are reviewed by a qualified language reviewer.

- Both versions are tested before launch.

**Policy foundation**

- **Official Languages Act.** “English and French are the languages of work in all federal institutions, and employees of all federal institutions have the right to use either official language in accordance with this Part” (Official Languages Act, 1985, s. 34(1)). Institutions must “make available in both official languages to employees of the institution (i) services that are provided to employees… and (ii) regularly and widely used documentation or other work instruments” (s. 36(1)(a)). Section 36 does not use the word “training”; DND policy applies it to training explicitly.

- **DAOD 5039-0.** The work environment must accommodate either official language, which “affects senior management, supervision, meetings, correspondence and work instruments, and the provision of personal services, central services, training and professional development” (National Defence, 2024, para. 3.5).

- **DAOD 5039-6 (training in both official languages).**

  - “As the CAF is subject to the Official Languages Act, IT&E must be provided to CAF members in the OL of their choice to promote equal career opportunities and optimize course success rates” (National Defence, 2022, para. 5.1).

  - IT&E counts as available in both languages if “all IT&E instructional materials, examinations and tools developed or acquired by the CAF, or produced on behalf of the CAF by a third party, are available in both OLs and are of equal quality” (para. 5.4).

  - “Course material for any course under development must be available in both OLs before the course is delivered” (para. 5.12).

  - “Online training that the CAF mandates or provides must always be offered in both OLs” (para. 6.5).

**Assessment equivalence across languages.** A translated test must measure the same competence with the same difficulty. The International Test Commission’s guidelines direct developers to “Minimize the influence of any cultural and linguistic differences that are irrelevant to the intended uses of the test in the populations of interest” (International Test Commission \[ITC\], 2017, p. 10, Guideline PC-3) and to “Provide evidence that the test instructions and item content have similar meaning for all intended populations” (ITC, 2017, p. 13, Guideline TD-3). The *Standards* likewise call for “minimizing the linguistic load of test items intended to measure constructs other than competencies in the language in which the test is administered” (AERA et al., 2014, p. 58). Research on linguistic simplification found that modified items produced slightly higher scores, with larger gains for English-language learners and lower-achieving groups (Abedi & Lord, 2001; paraphrased).

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** DND’s operative standard is “equal quality”, not merely “translated”. A French version produced late, with weaker narration, truncated text or harder test items, fails that standard. In assessment terms, any difference in difficulty caused by language is construct-irrelevant variance (see criterion 4.3): the test would then measure language rather than competence.

**Strength of evidence.** Policy-based and binding for the CAF.

**Reviewer prompts**

- Were both versions developed in parallel and reviewed by a qualified reviewer in each language?

- Are the assessment items equivalent in meaning and difficulty, not just literally translated?

- Is the French version complete and tested on the same date as the English version?

**Model note.** *The French storyboard was developed in parallel, uses Defence Terminology Bank terms, and its items were reviewed for equivalent difficulty by a bilingual SME. Reuse as a model of DAOD 5039-6 “equal quality” (National Defence, 2022).*

### 5.6 Development clarity

**Criterion.** The storyboard provides sufficient direction for Development without requiring instructional decisions to be invented.

**CFITES link.** The Design-to-Development handoff and the *Systematic Process* principle (National Defence, 2025b, para. 4.11). In the CFITES model, each phase’s outputs are the next phase’s inputs; for example, “The input to the conduct phase is the training plan that describes the learning programme and the instructional materials that enable learners to achieve POs/EOs” (Department of National Defence, 2002, p. 2).

**What good practice looks like.** Each storyboard screen specifies:

1.  The EO and teaching point it serves.

2.  Final, approved on-screen text and narration script in both languages.

3.  Each media asset and its instructional function.

4.  Interaction logic for every branch, the feedback text for every option, and the scoring and retry rules.

5.  Accessibility notes: alt text, reading and focus order, keyboard alternatives, captions.

6.  Navigation, completion and tracking rules (for example, SCORM or xAPI reporting).

7.  Version number and change history.

**Foundation**

- **Systems logic.** A systematic process depends on complete outputs at each phase. When developers must invent feedback text, distractors or branching, instructional decisions move to people who were not part of the analysis, and alignment with the objectives (criterion 2.3) can drift.

- **Prototyping.** Rapid prototyping, building and testing early working versions, was proposed as a way to surface design problems that written specifications miss (Tripp & Bichelmeyer, 1990; paraphrased). A short functional prototype of complex interactions complements the storyboard.

**Strength of evidence.** Practice-based. The justification is the CFITES systematic-process principle and professional practice, not learning experiments; cite it that way.

**Reviewer prompts**

- Could a developer build this screen without contacting the designer?

- Is the feedback for every response option written out?

- Are scoring, mastery and retry rules explicit?

**Model note.** *Storyboard Module 2 specifies the EO for each screen, full bilingual feedback text for every option, keyboard alternatives and scoring rules, so Development raised no instructional queries. Reuse as the storyboard standard (CFITES Systematic Process principle).*

## Domain 6: Quality Assurance and Continuous Improvement

Domain 6 checks whether the product has been tested before release and can be evaluated and improved after it. It puts into practice two CFITES principles, *Optimum Efficiency* and *Continuous Improvement* (National Defence, 2025b, para. 4.11), and the Evaluation and Validation phases. The evidence here is a mix of formative-evaluation research, usability science, training-evaluation research and quality-management practice.

### 6.1 Specialist review

**Criterion.** Appropriate subject-matter, instructional-design, accessibility, language, and technical perspectives are incorporated.

**CFITES link.** Development (Vol. 5) and the *Systematic Process* principle. DAOD 5039-6’s “equal quality” requirement for both language versions (National Defence, 2022, para. 5.4) implies a qualified language review.

**What good practice looks like**

| Perspective                   | Typical defects it catches                                         | Evidence of review                              |
|-------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------|
| Subject-matter expert         | Inaccurate, outdated or unsupported content; unrealistic scenarios | Signed SME review against cited references      |
| Instructional design          | Misalignment, weak practice, poor feedback, overload               | ID review against this checklist                |
| Accessibility                 | Missing alt text, keyboard traps, uncaptioned media                | Accessibility check against WCAG 2.1 AA         |
| Language (English and French) | Translation errors, inconsistent terminology, unequal quality      | Review by a qualified reviewer in each language |
| Technical                     | Broken navigation, tracking or scoring errors in the LMS           | Test report on the target LMS and browsers      |

**Theoretical foundation**

- **Formative evaluation.** Scriven distinguished evaluation that improves a programme while it is being developed from “terminal evaluation (we might call it summative as opposed to formative evaluation)” (Scriven, 1966, p. 5). Tessmer’s model of formative evaluation begins with expert review, followed by one-to-one, small-group and field-test evaluation (Tessmer, 1993; paraphrased).

- **Language expertise.** For translated assessments, developers should “Ensure that the translation and adaptation processes consider linguistic, psychological, and cultural differences in the intended populations through the choice of experts with relevant expertise” (ITC, 2017, p. 11, Guideline TD-1).

- **Accessibility expertise.** New federal regulations will require staff involved in ICT “development, maintenance or procurement” to receive “training on accessibility fundamentals … including refresher training at least once every three years” (Regulations Amending the Accessible Canada Regulations, 2025, s. 19.8(1)). The Accessible Canada Act also states that “persons with disabilities must be involved in the development and design of laws, policies, programs, services and structures” (Accessible Canada Act, 2019, s. 6(f)).

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** Each perspective catches a different class of defect, and no single reviewer can cover them all. Expert review is the cheapest stage of formative evaluation because it happens before learners are involved. Documenting each review also creates the audit trail that criterion 6.4 needs.

**Strength of evidence.** Practice-based, supported by the formative-evaluation literature and by language and accessibility requirements.

**Reviewer prompts**

- Which perspectives reviewed this version, and is each review documented?

- Were SME comments checked against the approved references?

- Was the French version reviewed as thoroughly as the English?

**Model note.** *Version 0.9 was reviewed by an SME, an ID, an accessibility specialist, both language reviewers and the LMS technician, each logged in the review tracker. Reuse as the review model (Tessmer, 1993; ITC, 2017).*

### 6.2 Learner trial and usability

**Criterion.** Representative learners are used, where appropriate, to identify strengths, barriers, and improvement opportunities.

**CFITES link.** Development and Evaluation: a trial with members of the target population before release, followed by programme evaluation (National Defence, 2009b).

**What good practice looks like**

- A small number of learners from the target population work through the module while thinking aloud, before full release.

- At least one participant uses assistive technology or represents a known accessibility need, where possible.

- Assessment items are tried out to see whether learners answer by using the skill, or by guessing, clues or misreading.

- Findings are logged, fixed and re-tested.

**Theoretical foundation**

- **Staged learner tryouts.** Tessmer’s stages after expert review are one-to-one evaluation, small-group evaluation and field testing (Tessmer, 1993; paraphrased).

- **Usability.** Usability is the “extent to which a system, product or service can be used by specified users to achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction in a specified context of use” (International Organization for Standardization \[ISO\], 2018, clause 3.1.1). The definition requires *specified users* in a *specified context*: representative learners on real systems.

- **Small iterative tests.** Nielsen reported that “After the first study with five participants has found 85% of the usability problems”, further effort is better spent on another round after fixes (Nielsen, 2000). This estimate is based on a model of problem discovery (Nielsen & Landauer, 1993) and applies to finding problems, not to measuring learning.

- **Response-process evidence.** The *Standards* list “Evidence Based on Response Processes” as a source of validity evidence (AERA et al., 2014, p. 15). Watching learners answer items is a direct way to collect it.

- **Involving persons with disabilities.** “Persons with disabilities must be involved in the development and design of laws, policies, programs, services and structures” (Accessible Canada Act, 2019, s. 6(f)).

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** Designers and SMEs know the content too well to see where novices struggle. Representative learners reveal confusing instructions, navigation problems and unclear items that expert review misses. Small, repeated trials find most problems at low cost.

**Strength of evidence.** Moderate. Usability and formative-evaluation research support it; the optimal number of trial learners depends on the product.

**Reviewer prompts**

- Were trial participants drawn from the actual target population?

- What changed as a result of the trial?

- Were assessment items checked for clues and misreadings?

**Model note.** *Five members of the target trade completed a think-aloud trial; three navigation issues and two ambiguous items were fixed and re-tested. Reuse as the trial model (ISO, 2018; Nielsen, 2000).*

### 6.3 Evaluation and transfer readiness

**Criterion.** The design supports later review of learning achievement and application to workplace performance.

**CFITES link.** Evaluation (Vol. 11, programme evaluation) and Validation (Vol. 8). “Program Evaluation assesses the merit or value of an instructional program. It is a systematic process designed to collect data to assess whether instruction has satisfied the objectives of the instructional programme in the most effective and efficient manner” (National Defence, 2009b, para. 3). “Validation seeks to determine whether the objectives of IT&E satisfy the job-performance requirement for which the program was initially produced” (National Defence, 2009c, para. 4).

**What good practice looks like**

- Results are recorded at objective level (LMS or xAPI), so evaluators can see which EOs learners struggle with.

- The end-of-course survey asks about job relevance and usefulness, not only satisfaction.

- The observable on-the-job behaviours each PO should produce are named, so supervisors can be asked about them later.

- The design states the organizational result the training should support.

**Theoretical foundation**

- **Four levels.** The Kirkpatrick model defines Level 1 Reaction, Level 2 Learning, Level 3 Behavior and Level 4 Results. Level 3 “measures the degree to which the target audience performs the critical behaviors in its environment and is supported in and accountable for its performance” (Kirkpatrick Partners, n.d.). The model’s authors stress “the importance of planning from Level 4 to Level 1 (or starting with outcomes and working backward to design for them)” (Kirkpatrick Partners, n.d.).

- **Reactions are not learning.** A meta-analysis found that “Utility-type reaction measures were more strongly related to learning or on-the-job performance (transfer) than affective-type reaction measures” (Alliger et al., 1997, p. 341).

- **A taxonomy, not a causal model.** Holton argued that “the four-level system of training evaluation is really a taxonomy of outcomes and is flawed as an evaluation model” (Holton, 1996, p. 5).

- **Learning outcomes are multidimensional.** Kraiger et al. (1993) proposed evaluating cognitive, skill-based and affective learning outcomes separately (paraphrased).

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** Evaluation and validation are only possible if the design creates the conditions for them. Objectives tied to job tasks give validation something to check; objective-level data shows where the course works; utility-focused survey items predict transfer better than satisfaction items. Kirkpatrick is useful for organizing evaluation questions but should not be read as a chain in which satisfaction causes learning and learning causes results.

**Strength of evidence.** Moderate. Training-evaluation research supports the practices; the four-level model itself is debated.

**Reviewer prompts**

- Will the data collected show achievement by objective?

- Does the survey ask about job usefulness?

- Are the expected on-the-job behaviours named for later validation?

**Model note.** *The course records results by EO, and the survey includes three utility items (“I will use this procedure in my next task”). Reuse as a model of evaluation-ready design (Alliger et al., 1997; National Defence, 2009c).*

### 6.4 Revision and change control

**Criterion.** Review findings and design changes can be documented and applied consistently across the product.

**CFITES link.** The *Continuous Improvement* principle (National Defence, 2025b, para. 4.11). CAF control documents are maintained over time; one training plan notes: “This document is revised periodically, according to the Canadian Forces Individual Training & Education System (CFITES) process” (Canadian Defence Academy, 2013, Foreword, para. 3). Control documents must be maintained “in both OLs” (National Defence, 2025b, para. 5.8), so a change in one language must be mirrored in the other.

**What good practice looks like**

- A review log records each finding: location, reviewer, decision, change made, version.

- Storyboards and builds carry version numbers and a change history.

- Repeated elements (glossary, standard feedback, shared graphics) come from a single source, so one fix updates every instance.

- Every change is applied to both language versions and re-tested.

**Foundation**

- **Plan-Do-Study-Act.** “The PDSA Cycle (Plan-Do-Study-Act) is a systematic process for gaining valuable learning and knowledge for the continual improvement of a product, process, or service” (The W. Edwards Deming Institute, n.d.).

- **Systems logic.** Because CFITES products are linked (QS to TP to materials to PCs), a change in one place can break alignment elsewhere unless it is traced and applied consistently.

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** Without documented change control, fixes are applied unevenly: one screen is corrected and its duplicate is not, or the English version is updated and the French is not. Change control turns review findings into reliable improvement and gives evaluators a record of what changed and why.

**Strength of evidence.** Practice-based (quality management and CFITES doctrine).

**Reviewer prompts**

- Is there a log linking each finding to a decision and a version?

- Were changes applied everywhere the element appears, in both languages?

- Was the product re-tested after changes?

**Model note.** *All review findings for Module 3 are tracked in a log with decision and version; shared feedback text is single-sourced, so one correction updated all 12 instances in both languages. Reuse as the change-control model (The W. Edwards Deming Institute, n.d.).*

### 6.5 Efficiency

**Criterion.** The design achieves the intended learning without unnecessary seat time, complexity, media, or maintenance burden.

**CFITES link.** The *Optimum Efficiency* principle (National Defence, 2025b, para. 4.11). The system aims to provide training “at the right time and in the most efficient and effective manner” (National Defence, 2015, para. 4), and CFITES is described as “a management framework designed to optimize the quality and quantity of IT&E, while minimizing the resources dedicated to IT&E programmes” (National Defence, 2009b, para. 3). Recognition of prior learning also supports efficiency: members should not repeat training for competencies they already hold (National Defence, 2025a; paraphrased).

**What good practice looks like**

- Content is limited to what the objectives require; “nice-to-know” material is cut or moved to optional resources.

- Experienced learners can test out or skip sections they have mastered, where policy allows.

- The simplest technology that achieves the objective is used.

- The product is modular and easy to update (single-source text, editable source files, no text baked into images).

**Theoretical foundation**

- **Efficiency defined.** Efficiency is “resources used in relation to the results achieved” (ISO, 2018, clause 3.1.13).

- **Instructional efficiency.** Paas and van Merriënboer proposed “a calculational approach for combining measures of mental workload and task performance that allows one to obtain information on the relative efficiency of instructional conditions” (Paas & van Merriënboer, 1993, p. 737).

- **Effectiveness, efficiency and appeal.** Instructional-design theory has long treated efficiency as one of three outcome criteria, alongside effectiveness and appeal (Reigeluth, 1983; paraphrased).

- **Less can be more.** The coherence principle shows that removing extraneous material improves learning (Mayer, 2014, p. 63), so cutting unnecessary content can raise both efficiency and effectiveness.

- **Time matters.** The online learning meta-analysis attributed part of the blended-learning advantage to differences in “content, pedagogy and learning time” (Means et al., 2010, p. xv), a reminder to compare designs at equal learning time.

**Interpretation: why this is good practice.** Efficiency means learning per unit of resource: learner time, development effort and maintenance cost. It does not mean “shorter is better”. Cutting the practice needed to reach the standard lowers effectiveness and is a false economy. The efficient design reaches the standard with the least unnecessary load on learners and on the organization.

**Strength of evidence.** Moderate (instructional-efficiency research and cognitive-load findings) and policy-based (CFITES Optimum Efficiency).

**Reviewer prompts**

- Which content could be removed without affecting any objective?

- Can experienced learners skip what they already know?

- What will it cost to update this module when the procedure changes?

**Model note.** *Module 1 removed two history screens unrelated to any EO and added a test-out option for experienced members, cutting average seat time from 50 to 35 minutes with no change in PC results. Reuse as an efficiency model (ISO, 2018; Mayer, 2014).*

### Proposed additional criteria

**A. Scoring consistency and defensible standards (add to Domain 4).**

*Proposed wording:* Answer keys, checklists and rubrics are complete and unambiguous; the pass standard for each Performance Check is justified and documented; and item pools are large enough to support retries on alternate items.

*Why it is needed:* No current criterion checks how responses are scored or how the pass mark was set, so the scoring and decision inferences of the validity argument (Foundation 3) are unprotected. The *Standards* require that “When proposed score interpretations involve one or more cut scores, the rationale and procedures used for establishing cut scores should be documented clearly” (AERA et al., 2014, p. 107, Standard 5.21). When cut scores rest on judgment, “the judgmental process should be designed so that the participants providing the judgments can bring their knowledge and experience to bear in a reasonable way” (AERA et al., 2014, p. 108, Standard 5.22). Rubrics with exemplars can improve scoring reliability (Jonsson & Svingby, 2007; paraphrased).

**B. Motivation and relevance to the learner (add to Domain 3).**

*Proposed wording:* The design gains attention, makes relevance to the learner’s own job explicit, builds confidence through achievable steps, and gives satisfying evidence of progress.

*Why it is needed:* No current criterion addresses motivation, yet trainee motivation is one of the predictors of transfer examined in the transfer meta-analysis (Blume et al., 2010, p. 1065). Keller’s ARCS model (Attention, Relevance, Confidence, Satisfaction) is the established instructional-design framework for motivational design (Keller, 2010; paraphrased), and UDL treats engagement as one of its three principles (CAST, 2024).

### Overlaps that are intentional

Some criteria look similar but check different things. Keep them separate, and use the scope notes in each entry.

| Pair            | First criterion checks                | Second criterion checks                 |
|-----------------|---------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------|
| 1.3 and 5.1/5.2 | **What** content is included          | **How** it is presented                 |
| 2.3 and 4.2     | Alignment of the whole design         | Validity of the assessment specifically |
| 2.2 and 6.1     | Accuracy as a property of the content | Review as a process                     |
| 1.3 and 2.4     | Nothing unnecessary is included       | Nothing necessary is missing            |

### Using the Overall Good-Practice Summary

The summary rows (“Strong practices to retain and reuse” and “Notable examples by screen or activity”) become far more reusable if each entry names the criterion number and the key source from this guide. For example:

- *3.5 — Each module opens with three retrieval questions on earlier modules (Dunlosky et al., 2013).*

- *4.4 — Option-specific feedback names the misconception and links to the review screen (Hattie & Timperley, 2007).*

- *5.5 — French storyboard developed in parallel and reviewed for equivalent item difficulty (National Defence, 2022).*

Entries written this way can be collected across projects into a shared bank of proven practices, each with its justification attached.

### Terminology

Since December 2025, DAOD 5031-2 uses the title *Canadian Armed Forces Individual Training and Education System* (National Defence, 2025b). Consider writing “CAF IT&E System (CFITES)” on the form and in formal documents.
